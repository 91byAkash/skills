# Copywriting reference

Contents: 1 principles and style · 2 clarity and message-fit tests · 3 headline formulas · 4 page structures · 5 section types and writing tips · 6 CTAs and microcopy · 7 page-specific guidance · 8 voice and tone · 9 awareness and sophistication · 10 claims and compliance.

## 1. Principles and style

- **Clarity over cleverness.** If the reader pauses to decode, you've lost them. Teams that measure clarity work report better conversion, shorter sales cycles, lower acquisition cost and more referrals, because the right buyers self-qualify faster.
- **Benefits over features.** Feature → "so that" → outcome. "Automatic bank sync" → "so your books are current every morning" → "close the month in two days".
- **Specific over vague.** "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes" beats "save time". Odd, real numbers beat round ones ("2,847 teams").
- **Customer language over company language.** Mirror words from reviews, calls and tickets ("drowning in spreadsheets", not "manual process inefficiency").
- **One idea per section**, building a single argument down the page ("rule of one": one reader, one big idea, one promise, one action).
- **Write to "you".** More "you" than "we".

Style rules: simple words ("use", "help"); active voice; confident, unqualified claims (drop "almost", "very", "really"); show outcomes instead of adverbs; honest over sensational (fabricated stats or testimonials are both trust-killers and legal risks); no exclamation marks in body copy; sentences mostly under 20 words with varied rhythm.

Devices that work when chosen deliberately: a question in the reader's voice that names their pain ("Tired of chasing approvals?"), analogies that make abstract mechanisms concrete, light humor that fits the brand and costs no clarity. Be direct: "Need to share a screenshot? Send as many files as you like" beats a long feature description.

## 2. Clarity and message-fit tests

**"Now you can…" test**: prefix the headline or benefit with "Now you can…". Keep it only if the result is both compelling and true.

Examples: "Now you can… have a powerful analytics platform" fails, because it describes the product without giving a new ability, and so does "…streamline your workflow", which nobody could disprove. "Now you can… see which companies are reading your pricing page" passes, and so does "…keep files, voice notes and comments in one thread".

**Discomfort → vision → path**: people act when they feel a current discomfort, can picture a better state, and believe a specific step closes the gap. The hero should hit all three: name the discomfort in their words, show the better state, present the product + CTA as the path. Missing discomfort = no reason to move; missing vision = no destination; missing path = no reason to pick you. A scheduling tool reportedly tripled hero conversion with a single line naming the small social awkwardness of sending a booking link, a feeling no competitor had acknowledged.

**Perception gap**: the same claim reads differently by risk tolerance.

Speed and novelty ("live by Friday", "a new way to…") excite early adopters and alarm cautious buyers, who read them as untested. Compliance badges and big-logo proof ("SOC 2 and 99.9% uptime", "used by three of the five largest banks") reassure cautious buyers and tell early adopters the product is heavy and not meant for them.

When a page serves both, split it (separate pages or a persona selector); don't blend into mush.

**Swap test**: if the line would work unchanged on a competitor's site, rewrite it; if you can't, flag `[NEED: differentiator vs named competitors]`.

**5-second test**: show the hero for five seconds; can people say what it is, who it's for and what to do next?

## 3. Headline formulas

Fill every slot with specifics; use any formula family once per page.

| Family | Formulas |
|---|---|
| Outcome | {outcome} without {pain} · {outcome} by {mechanism} · Turn {input} into {outcome} · {outcome} in {timeframe} · Get {benefit} from your {existing asset} |
| Problem | Never {bad event} again · {Question naming the pain}? · Stop {pain}. Start {pleasure}. |
| Audience | {category} for {audience} · {category} for {audience} to {job} · You don't have to be {expert} to {outcome} · The {adjective} {category} built for {audience} |
| Differentiation | The {category} that {differentiator} · The {opposite-of-usual} way to {outcome} · Finally, {category} that {benefit} |
| Proof | {Number} {people} use {product} to {outcome} · {Named result} in {time} |
| Curiosity (use sparingly) | What if you could {outcome}? · {Action} your {thing} like {admired example} |

Subhead: expands the headline with who/how/how fast in 1-2 sentences; often carries the proof number.

## 4. Page structures

Weak: hero → feature 1 → feature 2 → feature 3 → CTA (a list, not an argument).

| Page | Order |
|---|---|
| Full persuasive page | Hero → proof bar → problem → how it works (3 steps) → 2-3 key benefits → testimonial → use cases/personas → comparison to alternatives → case-study snippet → FAQ → final CTA with guarantee |
| Compact ad landing page | Hero (headline, subhead, CTA, visual) → proof bar → 3 benefits → testimonial → how it works → final CTA with guarantee |
| Enterprise/B2B | Outcome hero → recognizable logos → business problem → solution overview → use cases by role/department → security/compliance → integrations → case study with metrics → ROI/value → demo CTA |
| Launch | Announcement hero → video demo → 3-5 highlights → before/after → early testimonials → launch pricing or early access → CTA with real deadline |
| Comparison (vs competitor) | Who each is best for → summary table → key differences with evidence → migration path → proof from switchers → CTA (see seo-optimization) |

Length: match awareness and price. Low-awareness, high-price or high-risk offers need longer pages; most-aware, low-risk offers need short pages.

## 5. Section types and writing tips

Core: hero; proof bar (recognizable logos > many, real counts, star rating with review count); problem (articulate the problem better than they can; hint at the cost of inaction); benefits (3-5, each outcome headline + 1-2 sentences of how + proof); how it works (3-4 numbered steps, simple verbs, each with an outcome, time cues like "takes 2 minutes"); final CTA (recap, same CTA, risk reversal).

Supporting: testimonials (full name, role, company, photo; specific results and before/after context; avoid "Great product!"); case-study snippets (problem → solution → result with numbers); use cases and "built for" blocks (help self-identification); FAQ (5-10 real objections; doubles as search/answer content); comparison (vs competitors or vs status quo like spreadsheets); integrations; founder story/manifesto (why you exist, what you believe); demo/product tour; pricing preview ("from $X/month"); guarantee/risk reversal; stats section.

Problem-section openers (vary them): describe the specific moment ("It's the 28th and you're still missing 40 receipts"), then the time/money wasted, then the impact.

## 6. CTAs and microcopy

Avoid: Submit, Sign up, Learn more, Click here, Get started (unless nothing better fits). Use: action verb + what they get + qualifier. "Start my free trial", "Get the complete checklist", "See pricing for my team", "Create your first invoice", "Book a 20-minute demo".

- First person ("Start my trial") often edges second person; test it.
- One primary CTA per view; secondary CTAs visually quieter ("See how it works").
- Risk reducers next to the button: "No card required", "Cancel anytime", "Takes 2 minutes", "We'll never share your email".
- Say what happens next: "You'll get a calendar invite and a 3-question prep form."
- Form labels and errors: specific and kind ("Enter a work email so we can find your team") over "Invalid input".
- Empty states, tooltips and confirmation screens are copy; give each one job.

## 7. Page-specific guidance

- **Homepage**: serves several intents; lead with the broadest true value proposition; route visitors by segment or job; one primary CTA.
- **Landing page**: one message, one CTA, headline matches the ad/email; complete argument on one page; remove navigation where possible.
- **Pricing page**: answer "which plan is for me?"; one-line "best for" per tier; mark the recommended plan; FAQ on billing, limits, cancellation (see pricing-and-offers).
- **Feature page**: feature → benefit → outcome; show use cases and examples; clear path to try.
- **About page**: why you exist, what you believe, how that helps the customer; still include a CTA.
- **Comparison/alternative page**: fair, dated, evidence-backed; say who the competitor is better for.

## 8. Voice and tone

Define before writing: formality (casual ↔ professional), playful ↔ serious, bold ↔ understated, technical ↔ accessible, plus 3-5 personality traits and do/don't words. Keep voice constant; adjust intensity by element: headlines bolder, body clearer, CTAs action-led, error messages calm. Don't flatten a formal brand into clipped startup copy.

## 9. Awareness and sophistication

| Stage | Reader knows | Lead with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing about the problem | A story or symptom they recognize |
| Problem-aware | The pain, not solutions | The pain in their words, then the category |
| Solution-aware | Solutions exist | Your mechanism and why it's better |
| Product-aware | You, not convinced | Proof, comparisons, risk reversal |
| Most aware | Ready | The offer, price, deadline, CTA |

Market sophistication: in crowded categories where every claim has been made, lead with a new mechanism or identity rather than a bigger claim.

## 10. Claims and compliance

- Every number needs a source and date; every testimonial needs permission and must reflect typical results or carry a clear disclosure.
- Comparative claims must be verifiable and current; date comparison tables.
- Avoid "guaranteed" without terms, health/financial outcome promises, and "free" when conditions apply.
- Flag regulated categories (health, finance, legal, children) for review.
